    New here? Start with this

    This page shows two things for each coin or stock:

    • Trend — a guess at which way the price is leaning right now (up, down, or flat), based only on recent prices. It's a simple statistics calculation, not a prediction from an expert.
    • Accuracy — if you'd trusted that same guess in the past, how often would it have been right? Every table labels this against a coin-flip baseline instead of just showing the raw percentage — see below for why that matters.

    Use the Trending up / Trending down buttons above each table to filter the list down to whatever's currently leaning that direction. Nothing here tells you what to buy or sell — it's meant for learning how these signals behave, not for making real decisions.

    Why does a table say "63% (8 tries)" isn't meaningful?

    Flip a fair coin 8 times and you won't always get exactly 4 heads — sometimes you'll get 5 or 6, purely by chance. The fewer times you flip, the more a lucky streak can look like a pattern. The same is true here: with only 8 backtested tries, getting 5 right (63%) is barely more than plain random guessing would produce, even if the underlying signal has zero real predictive power.

    Each accuracy figure is checked against that coin-flip baseline using a rough standard-error calculation: sqrt(0.25 / trials). If the accuracy is within about one standard error of 50%, it's labeled "not statistically different from a coin flip" — which, on this page, is most of the time. A wider gap gets called "a weak signal", or rarely "unusual for a coin flip" — but even a wide gap on a small, recent sample is not proof of a real, repeatable edge.

    This is a simplification, not a textbook significance test — backtest trials come from overlapping windows on the same price series, so they aren't fully independent the way the math technically assumes. It's meant to honestly catch the common case (small numbers are noisy), not to make a rigorous statistical claim.

    New to options? Calls vs. puts, explained

    An option is a contract that gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price (the strike) on or before a set expiration date. To get that right, you pay an upfront cost called the premium — that's the price shown in the order form below.

    Call = right to buy

    Buy a call when you expect the price to go up. It gains value as the underlying rises above the strike, and becomes more valuable the further above strike it goes.

    Put = right to sell

    Buy a put when you expect the price to go down. It gains value as the underlying falls below the strike, and becomes more valuable the further below strike it goes.

    Premium
    What you pay to open the position. As a buyer — which is all this simulator does, it doesn't simulate writing (selling) options — the premium is also your maximum possible loss, no matter how wrong the bet turns out to be.
    In the money / out of the money
    A call is "in the money" when the underlying price is above the strike (and "out of the money" below it); a put is the reverse. An option that's out of the money at expiration is worth exactly $0.
    Why buy an option instead of the asset itself?
    Leverage: a small premium can control exposure to a much larger position, so percentage gains (and losses) are amplified compared to just holding the underlying. The tradeoff is time — if the move doesn't happen before expiration, the premium is gone for good.
    "Greeks"
    Real options traders track exactly how sensitive a contract's price is to different things — how much it moves per $1 in the underlying ("delta"), and how much it decays per day that passes ("theta") are the two you'll see labeled on your open positions below, once you place a trade.
    Example: a stock trades at $200. You buy 1 call, strike $210, expiring in 30 days, for a $3 premium. If the stock rises to $225 by expiration, that contract is worth about $15 (225 − 210) — a $12 profit on your $3 bet. If the stock stays at or below $210, the contract expires worthless and you lose the full $3 premium. That's the trade this simulator lets you practice, with fake money and real math.

    Glossary

    Every term below is explained in context somewhere on this page (in the Options section, the "New here?" box, or the Paper Trading panels) — this is just all of it in one place, grouped by topic, for whenever you land partway down the page.

    Options

    Call
    A contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying at the strike price on or before expiration. Buy a call when you expect the price to rise.
    Delta
    How much an option's value moves per $1 move in the underlying price. Labeled on the "Why did this change?" breakdown for each open option position.
    Expiration
    The date after which an option contract is worthless if it hasn't paid off.
    In the money / out of the money
    A call is "in the money" when the underlying price is above the strike (and "out of the money" below it); a put is the reverse. An option that's out of the money at expiration is worth exactly $0.
    Premium
    The upfront cost of buying an option contract. As a buyer, this is also your maximum possible loss, no matter how wrong the bet turns out to be.
    Put
    A contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying at the strike price on or before expiration. Buy a put when you expect the price to fall.
    Strike price
    The fixed price at which an option lets you buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying, regardless of where it's actually trading.
    Theta
    How much an option's value decays per day that passes, all else equal. Labeled on the "Why did this change?" breakdown for each open option position.

    Portfolio & risk

    Concentration
    How much of your total portfolio value sits in one position. This app flags anything over 25% as worth a second look — informational only, not a hard limit on what you can trade.
    Cost basis
    The average price you paid for a position — a weighted average if you bought at different prices over time.
    Disposition effect
    A well-documented behavioral tendency to sell winning positions quickly while holding losing ones, hoping they recover. The "Trading patterns" panel checks your own trade history for this.
    Realized P/L
    Profit or loss actually locked in by selling a position or closing an option. Unsold positions don't count here until you actually close them.
    Unrealized P/L
    The paper gain or loss on a position you still hold, based on its current price. Can change — or vanish entirely — before you ever sell.

    Signals & statistics

    Backtested accuracy
    How often the trend signal would have been right if you'd trusted it over recent history. Not a guarantee about future performance, and often not statistically meaningful — see "statistically significant" below.
    Potential mover
    A coin or stock currently near its recent low (within 10% for crypto, 5% for stocks) while also showing an upward trend signal — a naive "possible bounce" heuristic layering two already-naive signals on top of each other, not a prediction that anything will actually reverse.
    Statistically significant
    Whether a result (like a backtested accuracy) is different enough from pure chance — 50%, a coin flip — to mean anything, given how few times it's actually been tested. Most small-sample accuracy figures on this page are labeled "not statistically different from a coin flip" for exactly this reason.
    Trend
    A guess at which way a price is currently leaning (up, down, or flat), based on a simple statistics calculation over recent prices — not a forecast from an expert.
