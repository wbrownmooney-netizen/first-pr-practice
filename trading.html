    New here? Start with this

    This page shows two things for each coin or stock:

    • Trend — a guess at which way the price is leaning right now (up, down, or flat), based only on recent prices. It's a simple statistics calculation, not a prediction from an expert.
    • Accuracy — if you'd trusted that same guess in the past, how often would it have been right? Every table labels this against a coin-flip baseline instead of just showing the raw percentage — see below for why that matters.

    Use the Trending up / Trending down buttons above each table to filter the list down to whatever's currently leaning that direction. Nothing here tells you what to buy or sell — it's meant for learning how these signals behave, not for making real decisions.

    Why does a table say "63% (8 tries)" isn't meaningful?

    Flip a fair coin 8 times and you won't always get exactly 4 heads — sometimes you'll get 5 or 6, purely by chance. The fewer times you flip, the more a lucky streak can look like a pattern. The same is true here: with only 8 backtested tries, getting 5 right (63%) is barely more than plain random guessing would produce, even if the underlying signal has zero real predictive power.

    Each accuracy figure is checked against that coin-flip baseline using a rough standard-error calculation: sqrt(0.25 / trials). If the accuracy is within about one standard error of 50%, it's labeled "not statistically different from a coin flip" — which, on this page, is most of the time. A wider gap gets called "a weak signal", or rarely "unusual for a coin flip" — but even a wide gap on a small, recent sample is not proof of a real, repeatable edge.

    This is a simplification, not a textbook significance test — backtest trials come from overlapping windows on the same price series, so they aren't fully independent the way the math technically assumes. It's meant to honestly catch the common case (small numbers are noisy), not to make a rigorous statistical claim.

    What do these pattern names mean?

    Same reminder as above: these are naive shape definitions, not backtested signals — worth knowing what you're looking at, not worth trading on by themselves.

    Doji
    Open and close land almost exactly together, leaving a razor-thin body. Read as indecision, not a direction on its own.
    Hammer
    A small body near the top of the candle's range, a long lower shadow, and little or no upper shadow. Conventionally read as a possible bullish reversal after a decline.
    Shooting Star
    The mirror of a Hammer — small body near the bottom of the range, a long upper shadow. Conventionally read as a possible bearish reversal after a rally.
    Bullish Engulfing
    A down candle immediately followed by an up candle whose body fully covers the prior candle's body — conventionally read as buyers overwhelming sellers.
    Bearish Engulfing
    The mirror of Bullish Engulfing — an up candle followed by a down candle whose body fully covers it.
    Morning Star
    Three candles: a large down candle, a small-bodied candle that gaps below it, then an up candle closing back above the midpoint of the first candle's body. Conventionally a bullish reversal.
    Evening Star
    The mirror of Morning Star, flipped for a bearish reversal.

    New to options? Calls vs. puts, explained

    An option is a contract that gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price (the strike) on or before a set expiration date. To get that right, you pay an upfront cost called the premium — that's the price shown in the order form below.

    Call = right to buy

    Buy a call when you expect the price to go up. It gains value as the underlying rises above the strike, and becomes more valuable the further above strike it goes.

    Put = right to sell

    Buy a put when you expect the price to go down. It gains value as the underlying falls below the strike, and becomes more valuable the further below strike it goes.

    Buying a call is called a Long Call; buying a put is a Long Put — the standard names for these two positions, used on the order form and in your open positions below. "Long" here just means you bought it (own the right), same sense as owning a stock outright.

    You can also do the opposite: selling (or "writing") a call or put opens a Short Call or Short Put instead — you receive the premium up front rather than paying it, but the risk is flipped too. A short call backed by enough of the underlying you already hold is Covered (much lower risk); without that it's Naked, and loss is theoretically unlimited if the price rises far enough — this simulator doesn't model margin calls or forced liquidation, so a bad naked short can push your cash balance negative. A short put backed by enough cash to buy the underlying if assigned is Cash-secured; otherwise it's also Naked, with a large (but bounded, since a price can't go below $0) potential loss.

    Two legs can also be combined into one named strategy — the "Place strategy" picker below the Buy/Sell buttons opens both at once, priced off a single live quote: a Bull Call Spread buys a lower-strike call and sells a higher-strike call, capping both cost and upside; a Bear Put Spread is the mirror with puts, for a bearish bet; a Long Straddle buys a call and a put at the same strike, profiting from a big move in either direction; a Covered Call sells a call against stock/coin you hold (or this simulator buys the shortfall for you) for income, capping upside; a Protective Put buys a put against your holding as downside insurance. Each shows its own combined payoff diagram once placed.

    Premium
    What you pay to open a long position, or receive to open a short (written) one. As a buyer, the premium paid is also your maximum possible loss, no matter how wrong the bet turns out to be — as a seller/writer, it's the mirror: your maximum possible gain is capped at the premium received, but (for a naked position) your loss isn't.
    In the money / out of the money
    A call is "in the money" when the underlying price is above the strike (and "out of the money" below it); a put is the reverse. An option that's out of the money at expiration is worth exactly $0.
    Why buy an option instead of the asset itself?
    Leverage: a small premium can control exposure to a much larger position, so percentage gains (and losses) are amplified compared to just holding the underlying. The tradeoff is time — if the move doesn't happen before expiration, the premium is gone for good.
    "Greeks"
    Real options traders track exactly how sensitive a contract's price is to different things — how much it moves per $1 in the underlying ("delta"), and how much it decays per day that passes ("theta") are the two you'll see labeled on your open positions below, once you place a trade.
    Example: a stock trades at $200. You buy 1 call, strike $210, expiring in 30 days, for a $3 premium. If the stock rises to $225 by expiration, that contract is worth about $15 (225 − 210) — a $12 profit on your $3 bet. If the stock stays at or below $210, the contract expires worthless and you lose the full $3 premium. That's the trade this simulator lets you practice, with fake money and real math.

    Glossary

    Every term below is explained in context somewhere on this page (in the Options section, the "New here?" box, or the Paper Trading panels) — this is just all of it in one place, grouped by topic, for whenever you land partway down the page.

    Options

    Bear Put Spread
    Buy a put at a higher strike, sell a put at a lower strike, same expiry — a bearish bet that caps both cost and upside compared to a naked long put.
    Breakeven
    The underlying price at expiration where a position exactly breaks even — strike plus premium paid for a long call, strike minus premium paid for a long put. Shown on each open position's payoff diagram.
    Bull Call Spread
    Buy a call at a lower strike, sell a call at a higher strike, same expiry — a bullish bet that caps both cost and upside compared to a naked long call.
    Call
    A contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying at the strike price on or before expiration. Buy a call when you expect the price to rise.
    Cash-secured
    A short put backed by enough cash to buy the underlying if assigned. Lower risk than a naked short put, though still bounded rather than eliminated (loss is capped at strike × quantity minus premium received, not zero).
    Covered
    A short call backed by enough of the underlying already held to satisfy assignment. Lower risk than a naked short call — you'd hand over stock/coin you already own instead of having to buy it at whatever price the market has moved to.
    Covered Call
    Sell a call against underlying you already hold (this simulator buys any shortfall for you at the same live price). Caps your upside at the strike plus the premium, in exchange for income today; loss is bounded but not eliminated.
    Delta
    How much an option's value moves per $1 move in the underlying price. Labeled on the "Why did this change?" breakdown for each open option position.
    Expiration
    The date after which an option contract is worthless if it hasn't paid off.
    In the money / out of the money
    A call is "in the money" when the underlying price is above the strike (and "out of the money" below it); a put is the reverse. An option that's out of the money at expiration is worth exactly $0.
    Long Call / Long Put
    The standard names for simply buying a call or buying a put — "long" meaning you own the right, the same sense as owning a stock outright.
    Long Straddle
    Buy a call and a put at the same strike and expiry — a bet on a big move in either direction. Loses the most (both premiums combined) if the price sits right at the strike at expiration; gain is theoretically unlimited.
    Naked
    A short call or put not backed by the underlying (call) or enough cash (put). Higher risk than Covered/Cash-secured — for a naked short call specifically, loss is theoretically unlimited, and this simulator doesn't model margin calls or forced liquidation to limit it.
    Payoff diagram
    A chart of profit/loss at expiration across a range of possible underlying prices, shown on each open option position. Assumes holding to expiration; closing early almost always plays out differently.
    Premium
    What you pay to buy an option (long), or receive to sell/ write one (short). As a buyer, the premium paid is also your maximum possible loss. As a writer, it's the mirror — maximum possible gain is capped at the premium received, but loss isn't (unless the position is Covered or Cash-secured).
    Protective Put
    Buy a put against underlying you already hold (this simulator buys any shortfall for you at the same live price), as downside insurance. Floors your loss at the cost of the premium; upside stays uncapped.
    Put
    A contract giving the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying at the strike price on or before expiration. Buy a put when you expect the price to fall.
    Short Call / Short Put
    The standard names for selling (writing) a call or put instead of buying one — you receive the premium instead of paying it, and the risk profile is the mirror of the matching long position. See Naked/Covered/Cash-secured.
    Strike price
    The fixed price at which an option lets you buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying, regardless of where it's actually trading.
    Theta
    How much an option's value decays per day that passes, all else equal. Labeled on the "Why did this change?" breakdown for each open option position. A benefit for a short position, since it profits as the contract loses value.
    Writing
    Selling an option you didn't already own, creating a new short position rather than closing an existing long one. "Write a call" and "sell a call to open" mean the same thing.

    Portfolio & risk

    Concentration
    How much of your total portfolio value sits in one position. This app flags anything over 25% as worth a second look — informational only, not a hard limit on what you can trade.
    Cost basis
    The average price you paid for a position — a weighted average if you bought at different prices over time.
    Disposition effect
    A well-documented behavioral tendency to sell winning positions quickly while holding losing ones, hoping they recover. The "Trading patterns" panel checks your own trade history for this.
    Realized P/L
    Profit or loss actually locked in by selling a position or closing an option. Unsold positions don't count here until you actually close them.
    Unrealized P/L
    The paper gain or loss on a position you still hold, based on its current price. Can change — or vanish entirely — before you ever sell.

    Signals & statistics

    Backtested accuracy
    How often the trend signal would have been right if you'd trusted it over recent history. Not a guarantee about future performance, and often not statistically meaningful — see "statistically significant" below.
    Potential mover
    A coin or stock currently near its recent low (within 10% for crypto, 5% for stocks) while also showing an upward trend signal — a naive "possible bounce" heuristic layering two already-naive signals on top of each other, not a prediction that anything will actually reverse.
    Statistically significant
    Whether a result (like a backtested accuracy) is different enough from pure chance — 50%, a coin flip — to mean anything, given how few times it's actually been tested. Most small-sample accuracy figures on this page are labeled "not statistically different from a coin flip" for exactly this reason.
    Trend
    A guess at which way a price is currently leaning (up, down, or flat), based on a simple statistics calculation over recent prices — not a forecast from an expert.
